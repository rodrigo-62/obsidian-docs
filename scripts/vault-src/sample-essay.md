# <!--c:k3f9-->A Short History of Lighthouses:<!--/c:k3f9--> Light at the Edge of the Map

## Introduction: <!--c:v8d2-->Light and Distance<!--/c:v8d2-->

For as long as ships have sailed close to land, sailors have needed to know where the land ends. A coastline that is obvious by day disappears at night, and a harbour entrance that is easy to find in calm weather becomes a trap in a storm. The earliest answer was simple: a fire on a hill[^1], kept burning by whoever lived nearest.

{ZW}<!--c:m2p4-->This essay asks two plain questions: how did a fire on a hill become a tower with a lens, and why did it take so long?<!--/c:m2p4--> The short answer is that a light is only useful if it can be seen far away and recognised for what it is. ==Both problems turned out to be harder than building the tower.==

## I. Fires and towers

The first towers were little more than raised fireplaces. <!--c:e7q1-->Wood and coal burned unevenly, smoke hid the flame, and a keeper had to climb the stairs every few hours to feed the fire<!--/c:e7q1-->. On a clear night such a light could be seen for a few miles; in rain, hardly at all.

Oil lamps were cleaner but weaker. Keepers tried polished metal behind the flame to throw the light forward, and for a while every new tower used a slightly larger mirror than the last. The improvement was real but small, because a mirror can only send back the light it receives.^[Some towers burned whale oil, others rapeseed oil; the choice mostly depended on price.]

> {ZW}<!--c:q5b0-->A light that cannot be told apart from the others is almost worse than no light.<!--/c:q5b0-->
> — an old harbour saying

The saying explains why towers began to differ from each other: some flashed, some stayed steady, some changed colour.

## II. The lens

The real change came with the stepped lens: rings of glass arranged so that almost all of the lamp's light leaves the tower as one narrow beam. <!--c:r4n6-->A small flame behind a good lens could now be seen further than a large fire on an open hill.<!--/c:r4n6-->

1. The first effect was range: lights could be placed further from danger and still be useful.
2. The second was identity: each tower got its own pattern of flashes, printed on the charts.
3. {ZW}<!--c:t1w3-->The third was cost: lenses were expensive, so fewer, better towers replaced many small ones.<!--/c:t1w3-->

## III. Keepers and machines

For a long time every light needed a keeper. The work was regular and lonely: trimming wicks, winding the clockwork that turned the lens, cleaning soot from the glass every morning.[^2] Later, electric lamps and timers made the keepers unnecessary, and most towers today run on their own.

What has not changed is the purpose. A lighthouse still says the same three things it always said: here is the land, here is the danger, and this is which light I am.

## Conclusion

The history of the lighthouse is mostly a history of seeing further with less: less fuel, less labour, less guesswork for the people at sea.

[^1]: Coastal fires of this kind are mentioned in many old port descriptions; few of the sites survive.

[^2]: Cleaning mattered more than it sounds: a thin layer of soot could cut the range of a light by half.
